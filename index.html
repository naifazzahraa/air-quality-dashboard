Indonesian Urban Air Quality Dashboard

Synthetic Kaggle data · 1,200 readings · 10 cities · 20 Sep–20 Oct 2026. Choose filters; no selection means all.

Project goal

Compare air quality across 10 major Indonesian cities (AQI, PM2.5, PM10, dominant pollutant) and practice measure design and data validation in Power BI.

Data source

Kaggle “Indonesian Regional Air Quality and Public Health”: 1,200 readings from 10 cities in 8 provinces, from 20 September to 20 October 2026. Fields: PM2.5, PM10, CO, NO2, AQI score, air quality status, and dominant pollutant. The data is synthetic.

Analysis questions

  • Which cities have the highest average AQI and PM2.5?
  • How does AQI change over time?
  • What share of readings falls into each air quality status?
  • Which pollutant is most often dominant?
  • How do PM2.5 and PM10 relate across cities?
  • How do PM2.5 levels compare with the WHO guideline?

Model structure

A single flat table of readings (plus a calculated Year-Month column), so no table relationships were needed. DAX measures cover average and peak PM2.5, average AQI, share of readings by status, and PM2.5 relative to the WHO guideline. Visuals are linked through shared slicers for city, air quality status, and dominant pollutant.

Key findings

  • Average PM2.5 is 63.8 µg/m³, 4.25 times the WHO 24-hour guideline of 15 µg/m³; 99.8% of readings exceed it.
  • Average AQI is 79.0. City averages sit in a narrow range, from 73.3 (Palembang) to 82.0 (Medan).
  • Status mix: 42.6% Moderate, 31.8% Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups, 25.4% Good, and only 2 readings Unhealthy.
  • PM2.5 is the dominant pollutant in 55.3% of readings, PM10 in 25.0%, CO in 10.2%, and NO2 in 9.5%.
  • Peak PM2.5 reaches 110 µg/m³.

Limitations

  • Synthetic data, so findings do not describe real air quality.
  • Despite the dataset name, there are no health outcome columns (such as respiratory cases), so no health effects can be analyzed.
  • Only about one month and 1,200 readings, too short to study trends or seasonality.
  • Differences between cities are small and should not be over-interpreted.
  • The WHO guideline applies to 24-hour averages, while individual readings were compared against it, so the share above it is approximate.
  • Status categories appear to follow the US AQI scale rather than Indonesia’s official ISPU categories.
